Texas • systems engineering edition

Cheaper food through resilient abundance.

Operational definition: Texas food sovereignty is the capacity of households, communities, producers and institutions to secure affordable, nutritious food through diverse and durable supply pathways—without requiring autarky, ecological depletion, or dependence on a single retailer, crop, aquifer, technology or funding stream.

Baseline

Why Texas needs a portfolio, not one silver bullet

Texas is simultaneously a major agricultural producer, a fast-growing consumer market, and a state with sharply different water, soil and climate regimes. Price relief therefore depends on matching interventions to place and bottleneck.

1. Reduce avoidable cost before adding production

Attack spoilage, empty truck miles, fragmented purchasing, weak demand forecasting, small-lot freight, storage gaps and retail access friction. These can change delivered cost faster than waiting for new acreage or megaprojects.

deploy now

2. Add production where water + market fit

Favor crops and systems with high nutrition or dollar value per unit of scarce water, particularly near demand centers. In water-rich zones, expand rain-fed and perennial pathways without copying West Texas constraints.

place-specific

3. Treat redundancy as infrastructure

Regional food hubs, shared cold storage, alternative suppliers, school/institutional buyers, home-scale production and flexible microenterprises make the network less brittle when weather, freight or prices spike.

network effect
Important distinction: lower farm-gate prices do not automatically create lower grocery prices. USDA notes that processing, distribution, retailing and other post-farm costs account for much of the shelf price. The OS therefore optimizes the whole chain rather than only yields.

Bioregional operating map

Ten natural regions, ten different food strategies

Filter by operating condition. The point is not to assign one crop to a whole region, but to prevent statewide programs from assuming identical rainfall, soils, heat exposure or water sources.

Pineywoods

36–50 in rain

Fit: rain-fed forage, diversified horticulture, agroforestry, pasture systems, small-farm aggregation.

  • Exploit rainfall before pumping; prioritize drainage and disease management.
  • Pair producers with East Texas aggregation, cooling and institutional demand.
  • Use tree-crop/forage mosaics where land, soils and economics fit.

Gulf Prairies & Marshes

30–50 in rain

Fit: long-season horticulture, rice-region diversification, coastal greenhouse/covered systems, aquaculture where permitted.

  • Design for flooding, hurricanes, salinity and power outages.
  • Elevate electrical/cold-chain equipment and create backup cooling.
  • Use drainage and salt monitoring as production infrastructure.

Post Oak Savannah

28–40 in rain

Fit: pasture, mixed farms, vegetables, orchards and peri-urban supply.

  • Leverage mixed land uses for crop–livestock nutrient cycling.
  • Use small hubs to aggregate farms that cannot fill full trucks alone.
  • Build demand links to nearby metros and institutions.

Blackland Prairie

fertile clays

Fit: high-value peri-urban horticulture, grain/forage, aggregation, urban food infrastructure near the I‑35 growth corridor.

  • Protect productive soils from needless fragmentation where feasible.
  • Use metro proximity to shorten time-to-customer for perishables.
  • Design around heavy clay drainage, compaction and heat.

Cross Timbers

variable moisture

Fit: mixed livestock/crops, drought-flexible horticulture, DFW-serving production.

  • Use soil-moisture sensing and drought contingency cropping.
  • Create suburban food hubs with standardized producer onboarding.
  • Match crop calendars to institutional and neighborhood demand.

South Texas Plains

20–32 in rain

Fit: heat-adapted vegetables, legumes, grazing, shade structures and precise irrigation.

  • Use reflective shade, protected nurseries and heat-tolerant cultivars.
  • Prioritize low-pressure drip/subsurface drip where soil and crop justify it.
  • Test drought crops such as cowpea/tepary-type legumes before scaling.

Edwards Plateau

15–34 in rain

Fit: grazing, carefully sited orchards/horticulture, rain capture, spring/aquifer-sensitive production.

  • Protect infiltration and avoid strategies that externalize cost to springs/aquifers.
  • Use grazing management and soil cover to slow runoff.
  • Favor high-value crops only where water budgets close.

Rolling Plains

20–28 in rain

Fit: wheat/cattle systems, sorghum/forage diversification, deficit-irrigation strategies.

  • Use integrated crop–livestock rotations to spread weather and price risk.
  • Shift from maximum yield to margin per unit of water.
  • Use weather-indexed planting and forage reserves.

High Plains

15–22 in rain

Fit: water-budgeted field crops, sorghum/forage, dairy/beef linkages, precision irrigation, high-value protected crops near towns.

  • Ogallala constraints make water productivity a primary KPI.
  • Use variable-rate/low-energy precision application and soil-water sensing.
  • Reserve scarce irrigation for crops or stages with the highest marginal value.

Trans-Pecos

~8+ in rain

Fit: protected high-value horticulture, grazing where ecologically appropriate, solar-integrated systems and carefully justified brackish-water pilots.

  • Do not use desalinated water indiscriminately for low-value staples.
  • Exploit solar resource for pumping/cooling only after whole-system economics.
  • Closed-loop nutrient/water systems are attractive but require energy discipline.
Water-first design

A food strategy that ignores water is not sovereign

TWDB reports that groundwater supplied 55% of Texas water use in 2020; roughly three-quarters of groundwater use went to irrigation, and the Ogallala supplied most irrigation groundwater. The 2027 State Water Plan is therefore a core dependency, not a side document.

Water hierarchy

  1. Reduce avoidable evaporation/leakage.
  2. Match crop and season to rainfall.
  3. Capture soil moisture with cover and infiltration.
  4. Apply irrigation precisely.
  5. Reuse suitable water where lawful/safe.
  6. Use brackish treatment strategically.
  7. Use seawater desalination primarily as broader municipal/industrial supply where economics and ecology close.

High Plains rule

Optimize net margin + nutrition + resilience per acre-foot, not tons per acre alone. Pair irrigation telemetry with crop price, yield response and soil moisture so each additional inch of water must justify itself economically.

Priority: measurement → scheduling → crop mix → infrastructure.

Brackish-water rule

Texas has large mapped brackish resources, but desalination adds energy, brine disposal and permitting burdens. It is most credible when it substitutes for fresh municipal/industrial water or supports high-value uses—not as an automatic solution for commodity staples.

case-by-case
Water decision tree
  1. Is rain-fed production viable? If yes, model drought frequency and storage first.
  2. Is fresh irrigation sustainable at the local aquifer/basin scale? Consult groundwater conservation district and regional plan data.
  3. Can precision application reduce withdrawals without reducing profit? Compare LEPA/LESA, drip, subsurface drip, variable-rate and deficit strategies.
  4. Can reclaimed or non-potable water safely substitute? Validate crop, salinity, pathogen and permitting constraints.
  5. Would brackish treatment release fresh water for higher-value needs? Include energy and concentrate disposal.
  6. Does the full lifecycle water budget improve? If not, reject the intervention even if it is technologically impressive.

Current legal opportunity: cottage food

Texas SB 541 took effect September 1, 2025, expanding allowable cottage-food categories, creating vendor pathways and changing registration/labeling rules. That creates a lower-capital microenterprise lane, but time/temperature-control foods and wholesale pathways have specific conditions.

effective law

Current caution: grocery-access financing

HB 821 in the 2025 regular session proposed a Texas Grocery Access Investment Fund but was left pending in subcommittee. This OS therefore treats a state grocery-access fund as a policy option/proposal history, not an existing program.

Role-specific operating manuals

What each entity can actually do

Choose a role mentally or search within this section. Each playbook includes data, actions, handoffs and proof-of-impact.

Household / family
Goal: reduce food cost volatility without shifting unsafe burdens onto households.
  • Track 20–30 staple prices by unit cost; substitute seasonally rather than brand-loyal purchasing.
  • Use a 2-week perishables plan and freezer rotation to cut avoidable waste.
  • Join or form demand pools for staple cases only when savings exceed travel/storage costs.
  • Grow high-cost, frequently used herbs/greens first; do not assume home-growing bulk calories is cheaper.
  • Use SNAP/WIC and nutrition incentives when eligible; household food security is an infrastructure metric, not a moral judgment.
Proof: monthly cost per person, discarded food weight/value, days of food buffer, diet diversity.
Producer / rancher / grower
  • Compute contribution margin per acre and per acre-foot; include pumping energy and expected shrink.
  • Publish harvest windows to buyers 4–12 weeks ahead; use standing orders before planting additional perishables.
  • Pool cooling, transport or packing with nearby producers if fixed assets are underutilized.
  • Use sensor irrigation only with explicit thresholds and post-season water/yield review.
  • Divert cosmetically imperfect safe product into processing, institutions or food recovery rather than default disposal.
Proof: margin/acre-foot, sell-through %, rejection %, miles per delivered unit, cooling time after harvest.
Food hub / cooperative / distributor
  • Standardize product specs, case sizes, labels, insurance and delivery windows.
  • Use cross-docking for fast-turn product; cold storage only where dwell time justifies it.
  • Route by density and temperature class; seek backhaul opportunities.
  • Publish buyer forecasts and producer availability in a common schema.
  • Charge transparent service fees so producers and buyers can compare hub value against direct delivery.
Proof: load factor, on-time %, cost/case, spoilage %, producer payout share, buyer fill rate.
Grocer / independent retailer
  • Measure shrink by SKU/day and use dynamic markdowns before spoilage.
  • Test local producer lanes where delivery consistency and food safety meet store requirements.
  • Use smaller, more frequent orders for highly perishable lines when freight economics allow.
  • Expose unit-price comparisons and lower-cost staple alternatives.
  • Consider prepared-food conversion for safe near-date ingredients where rules and kitchens permit.
Proof: shrink %, gross margin after shrink, stockout %, basket cost for a standard nutritious basket.
School / university / hospital
  • Forecast volumes by item and week before asking local producers to bid.
  • Simplify specifications where nutrition and safety permit so cosmetic standards do not exclude local supply.
  • Use lawful geographic preference or local-procurement tools when applicable and document price reasonableness.
  • Aggregate purchases across nearby institutions if it improves truck utilization and competition.
  • Align menus with Texas seasons to reduce forced off-season sourcing.
Proof: delivered cost/meal, Texas-sourced share, bid participation, supplier retention, plate waste.
City / county / regional partnership
  • Map food retail, cold storage, commercial kitchens, public land, freight corridors and flood/heat risk.
  • Identify “missing middle” infrastructure: docks, coolers, wash-pack, light processing, small logistics.
  • Make public facility access rules transparent and vendor-neutral.
  • Coordinate emergency food logistics with the same nodes used in ordinary commerce.
  • Measure travel time to affordable food, not just supermarket count.
Proof: residents within practical travel time, hub throughput, emergency redundancy, private capital leveraged, operating cost recovery.
State agency / water planner
  • Publish machine-readable water demand, food access and infrastructure data with stable identifiers.
  • Evaluate agriculture projects on acre-feet saved, economic output retained and ecological side effects.
  • Prevent incentive stacking from paying twice for the same claimed outcome.
  • Use pilots with pre-registered success metrics before statewide scaling.
  • Design programs that can survive a funding-source change rather than relying on one temporary federal stream.
Proof: verified unit cost of outcome, permanence, adoption after subsidy, water saved, administrative burden.
University / extension / R&D consortium
  • Run paired field trials by ecoregion, not only laboratory demonstrations.
  • Publish negative results and full energy/water/labor accounting.
  • Create open reference designs for irrigation control, greenhouse optimization and cold-chain logging.
  • Pair agronomy with market-demand data so “successful” crops have buyers.
  • Include repairability and operator skill requirements in technology trials.
Proof: replicated effect, total cost of ownership, farmer adoption, measured water/energy intensity, open datasets.
Investor / lender / philanthropy
  • Finance assets with measurable throughput: coolers, wash-pack, modular greenhouses, route vehicles, meters.
  • Separate concessionary public-good capital from commercially repayable portions.
  • Require demand evidence before financing production expansion.
  • Use covenants for maintenance reserves and replacement cycles, not only construction.
  • Track cost per durable unit of capacity created.
Proof: debt-service coverage, utilization, producer revenue retained, cost reduction delivered, asset uptime.
Community / nonprofit / food bank
  • Use purchasing power to create reliable offtake for local producers when price and quality fit.
  • Coordinate surplus pickup with temperature-control rules.
  • Shift from emergency-only distribution toward market-building where mission and funding allow.
  • Offer aggregation, freezer/cooler sharing or vendor onboarding as reusable infrastructure.
  • Measure dignity and choice alongside pounds distributed.
Proof: cost per nutritious serving, rescued-food utilization, local producer spend, repeat household access, spoilage.
Deployment sequencing

Parallel workstreams by lead time

This is an engineering sequence, not a political endorsement. It shows what can be measured or deployed under existing capabilities while longer-lead infrastructure is evaluated.

0–30 d
Baseline + bottleneck map. Price a standard nutritious basket in target communities; map producer supply, cold assets, institutional demand, travel time, water constraints and existing legal programs. Establish data dictionary and baseline KPIs.
30–90 d
Low-capex coordination. Launch pooled purchasing, harvest forecasts, route pooling, dynamic surplus exchange, cottage-food onboarding, school/institution supplier discovery, and temperature logging at existing storage points.
3–12 mo
Missing-middle infrastructure pilots. Deploy modular cooling, wash/pack, shared kitchens, neighborhood pickup, mobile markets, greenhouse/shade pilots and precision irrigation on measured sites. Require before/after cost and resource data.
1–3 yr
Network densification. Add regional hubs only where throughput data supports them; standardize procurement interfaces; expand local processing; connect water-planning investments to food-system resilience metrics.
3–10 yr
Capital-intensive transition. Replace depleted-water production patterns with economically viable crop/livestock mixes; scale agrivoltaics where trials win; deploy strategic desalination/reuse; protect high-value production corridors and resilient logistics.

Measurement

Metrics that prevent “more activity” from masquerading as progress

Track outcomes at household, network, producer and ecological levels. Never use tonnage alone as the success criterion.

Affordability

Access-weighted cost: basket price + realistic travel/delivery cost.

Producer viability

Network efficiency

Cold-chain loss, load factor, empty miles, order fill rate, average dwell time, on-time delivery.

Resilience

Supplier diversity, days of buffer, route redundancy, backup cooling hours, critical-node concentration.

Nutrition

Water withdrawals, soil cover, salinity, nutrient losses, energy intensity, habitat impact and aquifer/basin trend.

Zero-harm / anti-inversion

Guard against “food sovereignty” becoming a new source of fragility

The system should lower household burden and ecological risk, not merely relocate costs out of the accounting frame.

Failure modes to red-team

  • Water rebound: efficiency lowers per-unit water use but encourages more irrigated acreage, erasing savings.
  • Energy rebound: indoor farming saves water but creates a larger power bill and peak-load exposure.
  • Localism premium: “local” food costs more because logistics are under-scaled.
  • Infrastructure vanity: a new hub is built without enough throughput.
  • Single-buyer dependence: institutional demand stabilizes farms until one contract disappears.
  • Data enclosure: a private platform becomes the new gatekeeper.
  • Ecological displacement: solar, greenhouses or new production damage higher-value habitat.

Safeguards

  • Measure absolute water withdrawal, not only “efficiency.”
  • Publish total energy per edible kilogram and per dollar of nutrition.
  • Require utilization evidence before major capex.
  • Keep data exportable in open JSON/CSV-style schemas.
  • Use multiple buyers, routes and storage nodes.
  • Protect food safety, worker safety and biodiversity as hard constraints.
  • Use pre/post baselines and kill pilots that do not beat simpler alternatives.
Do not confuse sovereignty with isolation. Imports, interstate trade and national logistics are valuable resilience channels. The objective is to ensure Texans retain multiple affordable options when one channel fails—not to eliminate exchange.
